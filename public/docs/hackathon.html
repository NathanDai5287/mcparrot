LA Hacks 2026

This page covers the hackathon only: the judging day, how judges score projects, which prizes we aim for, what we submit, and how to pitch. The product guide explains what MCParrot does.

Event deadline and attendance

We are entering AI Hackathon 2026 by LA Hacks at UCLA. Devpost, the website where teams submit their projects, closes on Oct 18, 2026 at 12:00pm PDT. The whole team must attend judging in person to receive a score and win prizes, and there is no online option. Use the LA Hacks event website to apply, RSVP, and check the schedule.

Coding and submissions both stop at noon on Sunday, October 18. The organizers give no extra minutes, and a late project never reaches the judges, so submit a first version early and keep editing it on Devpost until noon. Find our table and set up by 12:30pm, stay in the building all afternoon, and go to the stage at once if they call us for the Top 5.

How judging rounds work

The first round works like a science fair. Each group of judges spends at most 5 minutes at our table: 2 minutes for our pitch and demo, then 3 minutes for their questions. We will give this pitch many times. The judging system compares each project with the others it has seen, like a chess ranking, instead of giving fixed scores, and it does not send extra judges to teams that rank high.

The organizers say that judges go partly on feel: how clean and convincing the demo is, and how well we answer questions. Judges also compare us with the projects they saw before and after us, so some luck is involved. The live demo and the video matter most. The Devpost page only helps decide between the very best science fair projects. Sponsor judges pick their own winners in their own way: some visit tables for a longer or shorter time, and some judge only from Devpost.

After the first round, the organizers review the top projects, and MLH checks them for cheating and removes any team that cheated. The five best projects that pass go to the main stage at about 2:15pm. There, each team gets 7 minutes: at most 3 minutes of demo and pitch, then 4 minutes of questions from 5 judges. About 100 judges each pick their top 3, and the most votes win. The best project in each track, other than the top 3, wins that track, and other prizes depend on separate judges, MLH, and the overall ranking.

Some judges agree to share their contact details after the event. If a judge holds out their badge and agrees, take a photo of it, but never share our own details during judging. After judging, the organizers send a form that needs both the judge’s name and the badge photo.

Choosing a Devpost track

Each project enters one of three main tracks on Devpost: Applied Intelligence, Model Learning, or Beyond the Digital. Applied Intelligence is our main track, because it rewards practical AI that improves everyday work: you teach a tool once, then use it from your assistant. Agentverse, from the company Fetch.ai, is not a main track; we tick it on Devpost like a sponsor prize, and it fits if we show your request turning into real action through saved tools. We must name our main track in the Devpost description.

What we must submit

We submit on the LA Hacks AI Hackathon 2026 Devpost page and add every teammate. Devpost asks each person for the email they used to apply to LA Hacks, and if that email is not on the organizers’ check-in list, they disqualify the project as not in person.

  • Track and prizes — one main track, plus every sponsor prize that fits and Agentverse.
  • Project description — what it does, the problem, our track, and any sponsor categories.
  • Public GitHub repository — the project source code, with documentation.
  • Demo video (2–3 minutes) — a walkthrough of the features and how they work, posted as a YouTube link.
  • Complete Devpost page — a summary, the technologies we used, and links such as designs and reference documents.

The organizers suggest a cover picture in a 5:3 shape and a short, clean video, uploaded early so YouTube has time to process it. A good description tells our story and what inspired us, what MCParrot does, how we built it, how it meets each sponsor prize, and what we learned. Their good examples give each subject its own heading, a full paragraph, and pictures or diagrams. Their bad examples give one sentence or only a list of technologies. Judges, especially sponsor judges, read the Devpost page during and after the science fair, so it must stand on its own.

The Devpost submission page, deployed at https://mcparrot.vercel.app/docs/devpost.html, holds the project description we submit.

How to pitch

The pitch slideshow follows these rules from the organizers. The main slides must fit the 2-minute science fair pitch, and the whole talk must never go past the 3-minute stage limit; extra slides are only for answering questions. Lead with the idea and the product, not technical detail, because judges ask when they want more. Show many pictures, diagrams, and numbers. Practice many times, ask friends to judge a practice run, share the talk between teammates, and speak with energy.

The organizers say there is no single formula, and they suggest two shapes for the talk:

  • Startup pitch — open with a hook that shows the problem, such as a striking number or picture; give a short overview of how we solve it and what we built it with; show two or three distinct parts in the demo and explain them; say what we learned and what was hard; and end with what comes next.
  • Demo path — tell it like a short film, from problem to tension to resolution, in time order, and explain each feature as the demo reaches it instead of starting with an overview.

When judges ask questions, stay calm and polite, listen to the whole question, and ask them to repeat it if it is unclear. Pass technical questions to the teammate who knows best, and keep answers short, because judges have little time. If you do not know, say so honestly and show interest, and never argue. Before judging, mentors, the #ask-an-organizer channel on Discord, and organizers in person can all help.
